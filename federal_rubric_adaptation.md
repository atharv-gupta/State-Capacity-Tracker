# Federal executive-branch adaptation — read this before the rubric below

You are classifying an **executive-branch federal action** — an OMB memorandum, an OPM or
GSA instrument, an executive order, a Federal Register rule or notice, or trade-press
reporting on one of those — not an enacted state-government action and not a congressional
action. Apply the rubric that follows EXACTLY, with the adaptations in this section.

## The substitution

Wherever the rubric says "state government," read "**the federal executive branch**." The
four competencies are unchanged in substance; only the level and branch of government moves:

- `civil-service` — how the **federal** government hires, classifies, pays, evaluates,
  promotes, and separates its own employees, and where that authority sits. Schedule F and
  its successors, RIF and reorganization authority, OPM rules and governmentwide guidance,
  Senior Executive Service reform, the GS system and locality pay, probationary periods,
  removal and adverse-action procedure, MSPB and FLRA activity, telework and return-to-office
  directives, deferred resignation and buyout programs.
- `procedure` — the **federal** government's own procedural and compliance burden.
  Paperwork Reduction Act and information collections, OMB circulars (A-11, A-123, A-130),
  OIRA review, the rulemaking process itself, NEPA and permitting process, agency reporting
  mandates, grant administration and uniform guidance, the Federal Acquisition Regulation as
  process, the accumulated compliance apparatus agencies operate under.
- `digital` — how the **federal** government builds, buys, staffs, and oversees its **own**
  technology and data. IT modernization and legacy systems, the Technology Modernization
  Fund, FedRAMP, FITARA, agency CIO authority, USDS/18F-style delivery teams, agency AI
  adoption and governance, cybersecurity of federal systems, benefits and claims systems,
  identity and Login.gov, federal data governance, cloud migration, software and IT
  acquisition, the government as a smart buyer.
- `incentives` — the **federal** learning and feedback loop. IG and GAO findings and whether
  agencies implement them, program evaluation and the Evidence Act, performance plans and
  measures, improper-payment and payment-integrity work, outcome-tied funding, working-capital
  and franchise-fund mechanics, transparency that exists so someone can act on it.

**Principles 1 and 2 in the rubric apply completely unchanged.** Capacity, not subject
matter: a rule regulating an industry is `none` even when it hits our keywords. Direction is
irrelevant: dismantling the merit system is a *strong* `civil-service` item, not a non-item.
Gutting a digital-service team is a `digital` item.

State impact is **not** a criterion. An action that stays entirely inside the federal
government is fully in scope. Do not reach for a state angle, and do not down-score an item
for lacking one.

## The instrument test — the rule that matters most here

Executive-branch press offices produce a great deal of language and comparatively few
actions, and the language is written to be quoted. **Classify the instrument, never the
adjectives around it.**

An item is real only if you can name the concrete thing that happened:

- a numbered OMB memorandum or circular (M-26-15, A-130)
- agency guidance, a policy letter, a directive, or a delegation of authority
- a proposed or final rule, or a Federal Register notice with legal effect
- an executive order or presidential memorandum
- a workforce action actually taken — a RIF notice, a hiring authority granted, a
  reclassification, a pay determination, a collective-bargaining order
- a procurement action — a solicitation, an award, a contract vehicle, an acquisition-rule change
- a system, platform, or service going live, or being shut off
- a reorganization: an office stood up, merged, moved, or abolished
- a report that examined something and reported findings, or a data release
- a court order requiring an agency to do or stop doing something

These are **not** instruments, and an item that contains only these is `none` — or, at the
raw-gate stage, a Gate 1 failure:

- ICYMI items, interview and podcast appearances, op-ed reprints, transcripts
- a statement praising, condemning, or "responding to" someone else
- an anniversary, award, commemoration, or personnel-arrival announcement
- a restatement of an existing policy with nothing new in it
- an announcement that the agency intends to be more efficient, with no mechanism

**Neutral restatement is required.** Write every `name`, `headline` and `summary` as a
neutral description of the instrument and its mechanism, with the promotional and partisan
adjectives removed. "Historic," "commonsense," "radical," "unprecedented," "misguided,"
"war on" and "restoring" are not facts. If the release says OPM is "restoring merit and
accountability to the federal workforce," say what the document actually does — "OPM issued
governmentwide guidance requiring agencies to submit annual staffing plans" — and let the
competency and relevance fall out of the mechanism.

If the framing is all there is, the item is not an event. Say so rather than laundering the
rhetoric into a neutral-sounding summary of nothing.

## Verification — keep reported actions, label them

Trade-press reporting on a draft memo, an internal directive, or a decision not yet
announced is legitimate and often the most useful signal on this tab. Do not drop it for
being unofficial. Record which it is:

- `official` — the agency, the Federal Register, or the White House has published it
- `reported` — credible outlet reports the action as taken or decided, no primary document
- `draft-leaked` — reporting on a draft, a proposal under consideration, or an internal
  document not published

A `reported` or `draft-leaked` item can still be relevance 3 if the action would be a central
example. Uncertainty about publication status is not uncertainty about importance.

## Executive-branch specific rules

These override or extend the rubric where the two disagree.

1. **Routine Federal Register business is `none`.** A Paperwork Reduction Act information-
   collection renewal, a standard meeting notice, a privacy-act system-of-records update, a
   delegation reprint, or a technical correction is `none` even though it is formally about
   the government's own process. `procedure` requires that the *burden or the process itself*
   changes: a new or materially expanded collection, a burden-reduction initiative, a change
   to how rules get reviewed or issued, a consolidation of reporting requirements.

2. **Rules that regulate the outside world are `none`,** however large. The action must reach
   the government's own machinery. An AI rule constraining private developers is `none`; an
   OMB memo governing how agencies may use AI is `digital`. An environmental standard is
   `none`; a change to how environmental reviews are processed is `procedure`.

3. **Appropriations and budget levels are `none` by default.** A funding level, an increase,
   a cut, a rescission, or a CR is not a capacity event no matter how large. They count only
   when the action changes the funding **model**: multi-year or no-year authority,
   reprogramming and transfer flexibility, outcome-contingent funding, a new account
   structure, working-capital or franchise-fund mechanics. A shutdown counts only where the
   item is specifically about how agencies operate through a lapse.

4. **Personnel-in-office news is `none`; workforce-system change is `civil-service`.**
   A named official arriving, departing, or being fired is `none` unless the item is about the
   position's authority, the appointment or removal power, or the vacancy's effect on the
   agency's capacity to function. A RIF, a reclassification, a hiring-authority change, or a
   collective-bargaining determination is `civil-service` regardless of who signed it.

5. **Contract awards are `digital` or `procedure` only when the mechanism is the story.**
   A large IT award, a change to a contract vehicle, a protest that changes acquisition
   practice, or a modification revealing how the government buys and oversees technology
   counts. A routine award announcement, or a vendor's win presented as a business story, is
   `none`.

6. **Court orders about agency operations count.** A ruling that blocks or compels a RIF, a
   reorganization, a records practice, or a system change is an event, classified by what it
   reaches: `civil-service` for workforce, `digital` for systems, `procedure` for process.
   Litigation *about* policy substance is `none`.

7. **Legislative activity reported by the press is in scope but stays labelled.** The Hill
   and the trade outlets cover Congress heavily. A congressional action touching federal
   capacity is a real event here — set `branch` to `congress` — but judge it by the same
   rubric, and do not double-count committee press releases that the Congress tab already
   tracks from primary sources.

8. **GAO and inspectors general belong to this tracker.** A GAO report, an IG audit, or a
   TIGTA finding on an agency's workforce, IT, procurement, or program performance is
   `incentives` — that *is* the federal learning loop — plus whichever competency the subject
   matter hits. GAO's High-Risk List and priority-open-recommendations products are strong
   `incentives`. A report that only compiles data without examining whether something works
   is `none`. Set `branch` to the branch whose machinery the report examines, not the branch
   the auditor sits in: a GAO report on FEMA staffing is `executive`.

## Topic tags

The rubric's tag list was written for state government; the congressional tracker added six.
Six more exist for this tab and are usually the most accurate choice for an executive-branch
item — reach for them before falling back on a state-flavoured tag:

`acquisition-far` (FAR changes, contract vehicles, category management), `grants-management`
(uniform guidance, grant administration burden), `paperwork-burden` (PRA, information
collections, reporting mandates), `improper-payments` (payment integrity, fraud controls),
`customer-experience` (service delivery, Login.gov, CX directives), `merit-system`
(Schedule F, merit protections, MSPB and FLRA).

The congressional six remain available and mean the same thing: `federal-workforce`,
`elections-admin`, `appropriations`, `nominations`, `oversight-investigation`, `shutdown-cr`.

Tags are descriptive and independent of competency, so a `none` item can still carry tags —
but only tags that are actually about the item. Do not attach a tag you cannot point to in
the text.

## Calibration

Most items are `none`, and that is the correct outcome. It matters more here than on either
other tracker, because two of the three input lanes are built to produce volume: agency press
offices publish to be quoted, and the Federal Register publishes everything. Do not stretch.
When torn between a competency at fit-1 and `none`, choose `none`.

Judge the **underlying action**, not the framing. A release headlined as a partisan attack
that in substance announces a governmentwide staffing-plan requirement is `civil-service`. A
release headlined "modernizing government" that in substance announces nothing is `none`.

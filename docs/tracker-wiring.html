Stream 4 · inputs
The lane comes from the source, never from the model

A FedScoop story about an OMB memo is news about an executive action. If the model picked the lane, trade-press coverage could pass itself off as the original document. So the source decides.

When a cluster spans lanes, the most direct source wins: executive-action 4 › rulemaking 3 › oversight 2 › news 1. The document beats coverage of it, and it also beats a finding about it. If OPM issues guidance in response to a GAO report, the event is the guidance.

How the Federal Register is narrowed
The full Register runs about 1,637 documents per 21 days, nearly all of it ordinary regulatory business. So there are 26 targeted queries instead: full coverage of the 7 agencies whose subject matter is how government runs (OPM, OMB, GSA, MSPB, FLRA, OGE, NARA), every presidential document, and an 18-phrase sweep across all agencies. That fetched 212 documents and kept 18 in the first window.
Why oversight gets its own lane
GAO is a legislative-branch auditor, so filing its reports as executive actions mislabels them. And at roughly 24 reports per 21 days, GAO outweighs everything else on the tab, so it needs to be collapsible. Agency IGs do not come through here: an IG audit reported by FedScoop arrives via the news lane, because the lane records how directly you are hearing it.

Stream 5 · inputs
A headline is not an article

On a Google News search feed, the RSS description is just the title plus the outlet name — about 85 characters — and the link is a redirect token, not the publisher's URL. So the pipeline decodes the token and fetches the real article before any gate runs.

Until 2026-08-31 it didn't, and the gates were judging headlines. One week produced five kept items nationwide and zero from any competitive race.

Names
The roster stores legal names; the press uses shorter ones. Queries OR in the initial-stripped and first+last versions — "Daniel J. McKee" returns about a third of what "Dan McKee" does. Nicknames can't be worked out automatically and need the per-candidate news_query override.
The cap limits a run, it doesn't choose
Entries are date-filtered and sorted before PER_CANDIDATE_CAP = 100 applies. Google returns results ranked by relevance with jumbled dates, so the old approach of slicing the raw list dropped 30% of the week — and mostly from the highest-profile candidates, who are the ones that return a full 100.

Stream 4 · pre-screen
Where the pre-screen applies, and where it deliberately doesn't

news — pre-screened, using the congressional lists plus 60 federal-only terms, 217 patterns in total.

executive-action — not pre-screened. About ten items a window, and the whole point of the lane is that a dull agency headline can hide a governmentwide document.

rulemaking — not pre-screened. The 26 Federal Register queries are the pre-screen.

oversight — not pre-screened. One feed, about 24 reports per 21 days.

The anchor rule
Outlets marked broad in the registry — The Hill, roughly 100 posts a day across every beat — must also hit one of 46 anchors naming an institution or a document: OPM, OIRA, MSPB, schedule f, federal register, login.gov. The capacity words on their own match half of general political coverage, on terms like "oversight", "accountability" and "AI".
What that bought
In the first 21-day window it took The Hill from 1,706 posts down to 13 candidates and 5 events, while FedScoop kept 20 of 45.

Stream 4 · gate 1 · haiku
Executive-branch press offices produce a lot of language and comparatively few actions

And the language is written to be quoted. So the gate that matters here isn't about who acted — it's whether you can name the thing. An item gets in only if there's a concrete document or action to point at.

Things you can name
A numbered OMB memo or circular · agency guidance, a policy letter, a directive or a delegation · a proposed or final rule · a Federal Register notice with legal effect · an executive order or presidential memo · a workforce action actually taken (RIF, hiring authority, reclassification, pay determination, bargaining order) · a procurement action · a system launched or shut down · a reorganization · a report with findings · a court order making an agency act.
Fails
ICYMI · interviews · podcasts · transcripts · op-eds · statements responding to statements · anniversaries and awards · an official simply arriving or leaving · restatements of existing policy · promises to be more efficient with no mechanism · explainers · vendor business news · routine paperwork renewals · standard meeting notices · Privacy Act reprints · technical corrections.
Kept and labelled, not dropped
Trade-press reporting on a draft or unannounced action passes as long as the action is specific. It gets recorded as reported or draft-leaked rather than official. A draft memo described before it's signed is often the earliest real signal.
Then rewrite it plainly
Everything that passes gets rewritten with the promotional and partisan adjectives stripped out — "historic", "commonsense", "radical", "restoring", "war on" are not facts. Each row carries the original document so the framing can be checked against it.

Stream 1 · cluster · sonnet
Grouping and writing only — no judgment

The prompt is explicit that this step must not decide which capacity an event touches or how important it is. That's a separate call.

Rules
Two rows are the same event only if they describe the same action underneath — same bill, same appointment, same layoff, same contract — not just the same topic. The same bill at different stages within one week is one event at its latest stage, with the progression noted. Every input row has to appear in exactly one event, and a row that matches nothing becomes its own single-row event.
The shortcut with a side effect
When a state has only one row in the window, the clustering call gets skipped and the raw fields pass straight through — including the Haiku gate's why_it_matters. About 86% of state events take this path, so most published lines were written by the cheap gate off the raw article, not by the Sonnet prompt.
Safety net
If the model drops a row from its own output, that row gets added back as its own event instead of being lost.

All streams · classify · sonnet
The one call that actually decides

The rubric goes in as a cached system prompt — it's identical on every call, so it caches — with the event in the user message. It returns three things and nothing else.

competencies[]
Zero or more. Most events match none, and that's expected. Usually it's exactly one. Two only when the action really is substantially about both: legislative oversight of a failing benefits system is digital (the system) and incentives (the oversight). A second competency has to stand on its own.
Principle 1 — capacity, not subject matter
Is the government changing how it builds and runs itself, or regulating the world outside it? Only the first is a competency. A law about an industry, or a contract about a technology, is usually none even when it hits our keywords.
Principle 2 — direction doesn't matter
An action that damages a competency is still an example of it, often a strong one. A state outsourcing its IT and hollowing out internal capacity is a clear digital event and scores 3.
relevance 1–3
3 a clear central example · 2 definitely an instance but partial or indirect · 1 loosely related, right at the edge · blank fits none. When torn between a 1 and none, choose none.
topic_tags
26 descriptive tags on a separate axis: 19 capacity tags plus 7 sector tags (data-center, tax-incentives, energy-utility, health-human-services, higher-ed, k12-education, child-welfare). Sector tags land on none events on purpose — that's how you watch a theme that isn't a competency.

Stream 3 · classify · sonnet
One call does the writing and the scoring

With no gate and no clustering, this single call is the whole judgment for a hearing or a bill. It writes the title, the summary and the why_it_matters, then assigns competencies, relevance and tags.

Title discipline
6–12 words naming what the thing actually is. For a hearing or markup, the agenda text is explicitly rejected as a title — name the committee's action and its subject ("HSGAC markup of federal workforce and rulemaking bills"). For a bill, its own short title is usually already right.

Within a competency: relevance-3 items first, then relevance-2, each group ordered by most-covered then newest. The cap applies to the whole list, not just the top-up. It used to take every 3 and cap only the 2s, which let one federal competency print nine items against a cap of five.

04

The one line the whole email hangs on

tracker/shared/wim.py — two rule blocks over one shared base, worked out from a blind A/B against 60 real events plus a second round on the 20 hardest. Re-run the comparison before editing; the workbooks in review/ are the fixture.

RULES

Enacted actions — state, congress, federal

  • Name something concrete from this event — the agency, the document, the dollar figure, the deadline, the named program — and give a consequence the title and summary don't already state. A line that would fit fifty other events is a failed line.
  • Don't restate the competency. The reader can see the chip.
  • Routine housekeeping tempts you into inflating it. Say the smallest true thing: who now has to do what, what it replaces, who's affected.

CANDIDATE_RULES

Governors ’26 — the mirror image

  • A blind round using the general rules lost 8 of 10 candidate rows: it stripped out exactly what a candidate reader wants. So here you may name what kind of governing action this is, and link it to the record, the office, or the cycle.
  • But that fix overcorrected. The substance is the subject, not the campaign — none of the 4 winners made the campaign the subject; 3 of 6 losers did. The race can appear only as a trailing clause. If the line still stands with that clause deleted, it's built right.
first concrete a record to run on positioning their campaign

Shared base

The five failures that actually happen

  • Stop at what's known. No speculation tacked on the end. If an em dash is followed by "whether", cut from the dash — the line is usually already finished.
  • Don't guess at intent. Say what they did and what it does, never what they're betting on.
  • Everything has to come from the material. No figure, headcount, duration or "X other states also…" that isn't in front of you. An unverifiable statistic on a public tracker is worse than a plain sentence.
  • Keep the register flat. Report, don't characterise.
  • 30 words or fewer, one sentence. Most good lines are 20–25. Never open with "This", "The move", "The measure". Never return empty.

Who actually writes it

Not always who you'd expect

  • State — 86% of published events are single-article, skip the Sonnet cluster call, and publish the Haiku gate's line word for word off the raw row.
  • Governors ’26 — 67% the same way.
  • Congress and federal — the gates never write one at all. A single-source event gets "", and the digest quietly falls back to the first two sentences of the summary. Only clustered events carry a real line.

05

Traps that are still live

Known, written down, and not yet fixed — the things most likely to make a stream look broken when it isn't.

A wrong extracted date is now visible, not dropped

tracker/state/pipeline.py · the gate

  • Closed 2026-09-02: Raw Events now carries ingested_at and the dedupe windows on it, so a back-dated row promotes instead of falling outside every window. 13% of the 428-row table (56 rows) had an ingest lag over 7 days and had been structurally unreachable.
  • The side effect: rows whose date the gate got wrong now promote too. One in the first window reads 2025-01-01 against an ingest date of 2026-08-28 — a 604-day lag, almost certainly hallucinated.
  • The web view sorts newest-first on date, so such a row lands at the bottom of the feed rather than nowhere. Sanity-checking date against ingested_at in the gate would catch it.

A straggler article still duplicates an event

state + congress + federal dedupe

  • Closed 2026-09-03 by splitting the one window in two. --days is now only the trigger window — which states have an article we have not seen. --context-days (default 30) is the wider clustering window a triggered state is clustered against.
  • A late article therefore meets the siblings it belongs with, even though they aged out of --days, and attaches to their event instead of minting a second row for it. A/B on a real pair ingested six days apart: at --context-days 16 it produced a new event; at 30 it reported +src against the existing one and cost no classify call.
  • Quiet states are still skipped before any call, so the wider context is free. Beyond --context-days a straggler still duplicates — widen the flag for a deeper catch-up.

Quiet is not broken

Every stream

  • Read the --dry-run funnel before suspecting a scraper. It prints fetched → in-window → pre-screened → passed, per source.
  • Member offices really do go weeks without posting, especially in recess. 11 of 29 congressional press sources returned nothing in one August window; all 11 checked out as genuinely quiet.

Federal items are dated by the action

tracker/federal/dedupe.py

  • A story published this week about guidance issued in May is dated May, and falls outside a 30-day view. The table keeps it, because dedupe uses ingested_at — but the default UI window hides it.
  • Storing the publication date alongside the action date is the fix. It isn't built because it would be blank on every backfilled row.

Nothing dedupes federal against congress

Cross-stream

  • Moving GAO to the federal tracker removed the case that was actually producing duplicates, but a committee press release about an executive-branch action can still land on both tabs. Neither pipeline stops it.
  • Related: instrument_type is shaped by press vocabulary, not legislative — a story about a bill introduction can come back as regulation-proposed. branch is the reliable signal for congressional items.

State prints all four competencies even when empty; federal drops empty ones. The state rhythm is the spine readers learn; the federal half would print eight "nothing notable" lines.
